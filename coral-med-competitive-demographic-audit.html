Part 1 · Primary Care CompetitorsSearch: "primary care doctor near me" · "family doctor huntington beach"
Landscape

Fragmented, but with one well-funded outlier

Primary care in Huntington Beach is mostly independent and hospital-affiliated practices — the kind of field a local, physician-led practice like Coral Med competes in well. The one name that changes the picture is One Medical (Amazon-owned), which has two Huntington Beach locations and markets aggressively on a membership model.

What this means for the campaign

Coral Med's edge against One Medical isn't going to be outbidding a well-funded national brand on generic terms — it's insurance-first messaging. One Medical is membership-based; Coral Med accepts Cigna, Blue Cross, and UHC directly. Leading ad copy with insurance acceptance is a real differentiator, not just filler.

Zocdoc showing up in the auction is normal and not something to fight directly — it mainly affects CPC, not conversion, since someone who clicks Zocdoc is still choosing a doctor from a list afterward.

Landscape

Saturated — this is the real competitive fight

Huntington Beach has at least 9 verified med spas with strong review counts (4.6–4.9★ average), several running visible promotions and per-unit Botox pricing publicly. This is a much more crowded, price-transparent market than primary care.

What this means for the campaign

Pricing is public and competitive across this market ($10–15/unit Botox is the going rate) — Coral Med's ad copy shouldn't compete on price without knowing where Dr. Moussa's pricing lands relative to that range. Physician-led is the strongest differentiator available: several top competitors are NP-led or med-spa-branded rather than physician-led, and "Dr. Ameer Moussa, MD" in headlines is doing real work here, not just branding.

Expect meaningfully higher CPCs than primary care — this segment has more players actively bidding, which supports keeping this campaign's budget separate rather than folding it into the $500 primary care number.

What this means for targeting

Primary Care: the 45–64 group is both the largest income bracket and squarely the "establishing ongoing care / annual physical" mindset — this supports prioritizing the Physicals & Annual Exams ad group alongside general Primary Care rather than skewing everything toward urgent/same-day intent. The 19.4% 65+ population also means Medicare-eligible search volume is real and worth the office's explicit yes/no on the setup form.

Aesthetics: 55% of the population sits in the 25–64 range with strong household income ($120K–$136K), which is the classic Botox/body-contouring/skin-rejuvenation buyer. Combined with 37% of households earning $150K+, price sensitivity is lower here than the median-income figure alone would suggest — ad copy doesn't need to lead with discounting to compete.

No major ethnic or language-targeting adjustments stand out — the population is majority English-speaking (69.7% White, 12.9% Asian, 19.1% Hispanic/Latino, with overlap) and doesn't suggest a need for Spanish-language ad variants at this budget, though it's worth revisiting if the Aesthetics campaign scales.
